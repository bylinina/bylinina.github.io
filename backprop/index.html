How a model learns

Gradient descent and backpropagation in 15 cards, with numbers you can check by hand. Move between cards with the arrows, the arrow keys or a swipe.

1 / 15

1 · The big picture

Training in one sentence

A neural network is a function with a lot of adjustable numbers inside it: the weights. Think of them as knobs. Training means turning the knobs so that one number, the loss, goes down.

For a language model, the loss is the surprisal of the token that actually came next:

loss = −ln P(actual next token)

P = 0.25  →  loss = 1.39 nats  (2 bits)
P = 0.90  →  loss = 0.11 nats

So training a language model means making real text less surprising to it, averaged over a very large number of positions in the training data.

That leaves two questions, and the rest of the cards answer them. Which way should each knob turn? That's gradient descent. And how do we work that out for millions of knobs at once? That's backpropagation.

2 · A toy model

A model with one knob

Strip everything down to one weight, w. The model multiplies its input by w:

model:  ŷ = w · x
        (ŷ, "y-hat", is the guess)
data:   x = 2, correct answer y = 6
loss:   L = (ŷ − y)²  (squared error)

At w = 1 the guess is ŷ = 2 and the loss is (2 − 6)² = 16. Drag the knob:

Here we can see the whole curve, with the bottom at w = 3. A real model can't: with millions of knobs there is no curve to look at. All it can compute is the loss where it currently stands, plus a local clue about which way is downhill. That clue is the derivative.

3 · The derivative

The derivative is a nudge test

Stand at w = 1. Nudge the knob by a small amount, recompute the loss, and divide the change in loss by the size of the nudge. Pick a nudge size:

As the nudge shrinks, the ratio settles on −16, and the crimson line through the two points turns into the tangent (dashed): the line that just touches the curve at w = 1. That limiting ratio is the derivative of the loss with respect to w, written dL/dw.

Read it as an exchange rate: at this spot, each unit added to w changes the loss by about −16. The sign gives the direction (negative: increasing w lowers the loss). The size gives the steepness; near the bottom it gets close to 0.

4 · The shortcut, part 1

The slope of a square

Nudge tests work, but every one costs an extra run of the model. There is a shortcut that gives −16 exactly, from numbers we already have. It rests on two simple facts. The first is about squares.

The loss is a square: L = gap², and the gap between the correct answer and the guess is 6 − 2 = 4. Now push the guess ŷ up by a small amount h. The gap shrinks to 4 − h, so the square loses area:

1

Two crimson strips of 4 × h are cut off. Where they overlap, the little h × h corner has been cut twice, so it's added back once. As h gets small, the corner stops mattering (0.01 × 0.01 = 0.0001, next to strips worth 0.08), and the loss changes at a rate of −2 × 4 = −8 per unit that ŷ moves up.

Rule: the slope of a square is 2 × the thing being squared. Here that thing is ŷ − y = −4, so dL/dŷ = 2·(ŷ − y) = −8.

5 · The shortcut, part 2

Through the multiplication

The second fact: ŷ = w · x with x = 2. If w moves by h, ŷ moves by 2h: twice as far, because w gets multiplied by 2. So the rate of ŷ with respect to w is just x.

Now chain the two facts. Follow a nudge of 0.01 through the model:

w moves by               0.01
ŷ moves by   2 × 0.01  =  0.02
L moves by  −8 × 0.02  = −0.16

L per unit of w:  −0.16 ÷ 0.01 = −16

Written as a formula, that is all the "calculus" amounts to:

dL/dw = 2·(ŷ − y)rate of the square
here −8 × xrate of the
multiplication: 2 = −16

Everything in it (ŷ, y and x) is already known once the loss has been computed, so no extra runs are needed. Drag the knob: the formula and a nudge test agree everywhere, and the crimson line has exactly that slope.

6 · Gradient descent

Step against the slope

new w = w − learning rate × slope

The minus sign does the steering: a negative slope (loss falls to the right) moves w up, a positive slope moves it down. The learning rate (lr) sets the step size. With lr = 0.05, starting from w = 1:

stepwŷlossslopeΔw

slope = 2·(ŷ − 6)·2  ·  Δw = −0.05 × slope  ·  rounded to 2 decimals

No one tells the model to slow down near the bottom: the slope gets gentler there, so the steps shrink by themselves. In this toy the gap to the right answer shrinks by the same factor, 0.6, at every step.

7 · The learning rate

Too small, about right, too big

Same model, same start (w = 1), different learning rates. Drag the slider and watch the first six steps.

0.05

Too small is safe but slow. Too big overshoots the bottom by more than it started from, and the loss explodes. In real training the learning rate is one of the main settings people tune, and it usually follows a schedule: a short warm-up, then a gradual decrease.

8 · Many knobs

Partial derivatives and the gradient

Real models have millions of knobs. Add a second one to the toy, a bias b, and a second training example:

model:  ŷ = w · x + b
data:   x = 1, y = 3  and  x = 2, y = 6
loss:   average squared error

w = 1, b = 0:  guesses 1 and 2
errors −2 and −4,  L = (4 + 16)/2 = 10

A partial derivative is the same nudge test, one knob at a time, with every other knob frozen. With the shortcut from cards 4 and 5:

∂L/∂w:  average of 2·(ŷ − y)·x
        = (−4 + −16) / 2 = −10
∂L/∂b:  average of 2·(ŷ − y)·1
        = (−4 + −8) / 2  = −6

The list of all the partial derivatives, here (−10, −6), is the gradient. It points uphill, in the steepest direction. Gradient descent steps every knob at once, against it. With lr = 0.1: w = 2.0, b = 0.6, and the loss drops from 10 to 1.06.

Tap the map to pick a starting point. Each ring is a line of equal loss; the black dot at w = 3, b = 0 is the bottom. The crimson arrow is the first step: against the gradient, at right angles to the rings.

The path drops quickly into the valley, then crawls along its floor. Long, narrow valleys like this one are common in real networks too, and they are one reason practical optimizers add momentum (last card).

9 · The chain rule

Rates along a chain multiply

Card 5 already used it. If gear A turns gear B 3 times per turn, and B turns gear C 2 times per turn, then A turns C 3 × 2 = 6 times per turn.

A×3 →B×2 →CA → C: ×6

A network is a long chain of simple steps. How strongly the loss responds to any knob is the product of the local rates along the path from that knob to the loss. For the one-knob toy:

step         local rate
ŷ = w · x    dŷ/dw = x  =  2
e = ŷ − y    de/dŷ = 1
L = e²       dL/de = 2e = −8
─────────────────────────────
dL/dw = 2 × 1 × (−8) = −16

Each step only needs its own local rate, and those are easy. A multiplication passes on the other factor. An addition or subtraction passes on 1. A square passes on 2 × what was squared. Nobody ever needs a formula for the whole network.

10 · Backpropagation

Forward, then backward

Backprop applies the chain rule to every knob in one sweep: run the model forward to get the loss, then walk back from the loss, multiplying local rates as you go. A toy with two layers and two knobs, w₁ and w₂:

Check by nudging: with w₁ = 2.01 the loss goes from 4 to 3.980, a rate of −2. ✓

w₂ gets the bigger correction because the loss is more sensitive to it (−8 against −2). Notice also that the backward pass reused values from the forward pass (h, w₂, x). That's why they're kept in memory during training, and why training needs far more memory than just running a model.

11 · Cost

Why backprop is cheap

The nudge test needs one extra forward pass per knob. Backprop gets every slope from a single backward pass, which costs about as much as two forward passes. Where does the "two" come from?

Nearly all the computation in a neural network is multiplying a weight by an incoming value. Take one such multiplication, out = w · a, and suppose the backward pass has delivered the signal g = dL/d(out) to it:

forward:   out = w · a      (1 ×)

backward:  slope for w:   g · a
           signal for a:  g · w  (2 ×)

Each multiplication in the forward pass turns into two in the backward pass: one gives the weight its slope, the other sends the signal further back. Additions just pass the signal through. In matrix terms, a layer's forward pass is one matrix multiplication and its backward pass is two of the same size. Hence backward ≈ 2 × forward, and a whole training step ≈ 3 forward passes, however many weights there are. This is where the usual estimate comes from: running a model costs about 2 operations per weight per token, training about 6.

The signal is also computed once and shared. In card 10, dL/dh = −2 served w₁; in a deep network, the signal arriving at a layer serves every weight below it.

12 · Language models

Scores, probabilities, loss

Back to language models. The last layer gives every word in the vocabulary a score, called a logit. Softmax turns the scores into probabilities: raise e to the power of each score, then divide by the total so that everything sums to 1. A toy vocabulary of three words, after Emma put her mug of …

word    score   e^score   P
tea      2.0     7.39    0.50
coffee   1.5     4.48    0.30
juice    1.1     3.00    0.20
total           14.87    1.00

P = e^score ÷ total,  e.g. 7.39 ÷ 14.87

Suppose the text actually continues with coffee. The loss is coffee's surprisal:

L = −ln P(coffee)
  = −ln 0.30 = 1.20 nats

Only coffee's probability appears in the loss. But that probability depends on all three scores: raising tea's score makes the total bigger and coffee's share smaller. So every score gets a slope, and the next card works them out.

13 · The learning signal

The slope for every score

Same nudge test as before: add 0.01 to one score, recompute the probabilities and the loss, and divide the change in loss by 0.01.

nudged   P(coffee)   loss     rate
(none)    0.3013    1.1997
coffee    0.3034    1.1927   −0.70
tea       0.2998    1.2047   +0.50
juice     0.3007    1.2017   +0.20

The rates follow one simple pattern:

slope for a word's score = P(word) − 1 if it's the word that actually came next, P(word) otherwise.
coffee 0.30 − 1 = −0.70 · tea +0.50 · juice +0.20

Why? The loss can be rewritten as L = −(coffee's score) + ln(total). The first part lowers the loss at rate 1 when coffee's score goes up. The second part, ln(total), responds to each word's score in proportion to that word's share of the total, which is exactly its probability. Together: P(word) − 1 for the correct word, P(word) for every other word.

Gradient descent subtracts lr × slope from each score (here lr = 1):

tea     2.0 − 0.50 = 1.50  →  P = 0.28
coffee  1.5 + 0.70 = 2.20  →  P = 0.56
juice   1.1 − 0.20 = 0.90  →  P = 0.15
loss:   1.20 → 0.57 nats
  • The correct word is pushed up by 1 − its probability. The more surprised the model was, the bigger the push; a word already at 0.99 barely changes anything. Learning is driven by prediction error.
  • Each wrong word is pushed down in proportion to how much the model believed in it.
  • The slopes sum to zero (−0.70 + 0.50 + 0.20 = 0): probability moves from the wrong words to the right one.

14 · Try it

One prediction, one update

Set the three scores, choose which word actually came next, and take gradient steps. The correct word's slope is always P − 1; the others' are just P.

Where the signal goes next

Each score is computed from the model's final hidden vector h and an output vector for that word: score = (word's output vector) · h. So the slopes flow two ways. Coffee's output vector is nudged toward h, and tea's and juice's away from it. And the signal for h says: move toward coffee's output vector and away from the others. Backprop carries that signal down through every layer, to every weight, embeddings included.

15 · Putting it together

The real training loop

repeat, thousands of times:
  1. take a batch of text snippets
  2. forward: predict every next token,
     average the surprisals → loss
  3. backward: a slope for every weight
  4. update: step every weight
     against its slope

This is stochastic gradient descent. Each batch is a small random sample of the training data, so its gradient is a noisy estimate of the gradient over all the data, but a much cheaper one. The loss is an average over every position in the batch, so the slopes are averages too: each position contributes its own P − 1 / P signal from card 13, carried down by backprop.

In practice, step 4 uses Adam or AdamW instead of the plain update. Adam keeps a running average of recent slopes (momentum, which helps in long, narrow valleys like the one in card 8) and scales each weight's step by how large that weight's slopes have been. The learning rate is still the main setting.

The tiny GPT from the afternoon practical has about 4.2 million weights. Every one of its training steps computed 4.2 million slopes this way, with one forward and one backward pass.
